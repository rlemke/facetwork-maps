Can a published climate normal be recomputed from the data it derives from?

Recomputing NOAA's 1991–2020 U.S. Climate Normals from the GHCN-Daily observations they are built on, across 12 stations, as a Facetwork FFL workflow. September 2026.

1. Purpose of the analysis

A climate normal is the reference against which "hotter than average" is measured — in agriculture, energy demand forecasting, insurance pricing and building codes. NOAA publishes the normals and the daily observations they derive from, so unlike most published results this one can be checked rather than trusted.

The question is not "are the published normals correct". It is narrower and more useful: can an independent party, starting from the same archived observations, arrive at the same numbers? Where the answer is no, the gap measures how much of the published product depends on decisions that are documented in prose but not reproducible from the archive.

The normals carry their own provenance: comp_flag records how each value was derived and years_* how many years contributed. For the stations here that field reads 30 — the full period.

New York's input is a clean control: 10,958 days, 100% complete, zero QC flags. Any difference there is methodological, not missing data.

3. What our FFL workflow found

Each station is fetched, recomputed (mean of daily values per calendar month, tenths °C → °F), and compared against the published normals. 12 stations, 61 tasks, distributed across a four-host fleet. 432 values in total (12 months × TMAX/TMIN/TAVG × 12 stations).

Reproduction quality is a spectrum, not a pass/fail. An earlier version of this page reported "four stations do not reproduce at all". That was an artifact of the threshold, not a property of the data — see §6. Sorted by how closely each station reproduces:

"Bias" is the mean signed residual (ours − NOAA) where an exact sign test finds the residuals systematically one-sided (p < 0.01 over 12 months); none means consistent with noise. The sign test is threshold-independent, so unlike the error magnitudes it does not move when the tolerance changes.

Los Angeles reproduces to 0.040 °F median; Chicago is off by 0.875. That is a twentyfold spread across stations processed from the same archive by the same code. Overall:

The single-station result was misleading, and the fan-out is what showed it. New York alone gives TMAX high in all 12 months (+0.242) and TMIN low in all 12 (−0.511) — a clean, consistent offset. The obvious explanation is a unit round-trip: US first-order stations record whole degrees F, GHCN-Daily archives tenths of a degree C, and the conversion does not commute.

Twelve stations refute it. TMAX bias runs −0.899 (Seattle) to +0.242 (New York) — it flips sign. An archive-wide rounding artifact would be uniform in both magnitude and direction. Los Angeles shows no systematic bias in either column, and reproduces to 0.04 °F. The difference is station-specific — consistent with per-station handling of missing days, station moves and instrument changes — not an artifact of the archive.

With n = 1 the wrong explanation was not merely possible, it was compelling.

The algorithmic point: a tolerance cannot see a bias

At New York every one of 36 values sits within a generous tolerance and every one falls on the same side per column — TMAX high 12/12, TMIN low 12/12.

verdict : agree at level `values`,  differing: 0
TMAX residuals SYSTEMATIC: 12+/0- of 12, mean +0.2425  (p=0.00049)
TMIN residuals SYSTEMATIC: 0+/12- of 12, mean -0.5108  (p=0.00049)

Agrees and differs at once. "36 small random errors" and "36 errors in the same direction" are completely different claims about a reproduction, and only the second says the method differs. A tolerance is a per-value predicate; it cannot express that. So the comparison runs an exact sign test over the signed residuals — Binomial(n, ½) under the null, no distributional assumption — and reports the structure beside the verdict. Being threshold-free, it is the part of this study that does not move when the tolerance does.

The test is per column, never pooled. The first implementation pooled, and TMAX running high cancelled TMIN running low into a balanced sign split reporting "no bias" — while both columns were systematically wrong in opposite directions. The check destroyed the signal it existed to find.

What the verdict levels mean

The comparison returns the highest rung reached on a ladder, so the level names what did match and thereby locates the first thing that did not:

levelmeansthe failure is
existsreadable, fields disagreewrong file, schema change
schemafields agree, record sets do notrecords added, dropped, or out of scope
cardinalitycounts agree, which records do notsame number, different members
keysthe same records exist; their values differthe numbers — a method difference
valuesvalues agree under declared normalisationsonly serialisation differs
bytesidentical filesnothing

keys is the informative rung and the easiest to misread. It says both sides looked at exactly the same population and computed different answers — nothing lost in transfer, no filter diverged, no schema drifted. That is a finding about method. A verdict of cardinality or lower usually means something broke before the science started.

The key columns define what "the same record" means, which is a substantive claim rather than configuration. Here the key is (month): twelve records, all twelve present on both sides, so every disagreement is necessarily at keys — the months are the same, the temperatures are not. In the companion GIAB study the key is (CHROM, POS, REF, ALT); without it records would match by position in the file, and one inserted row would misalign everything after it.

5. Reproducing this

fw ffl run --primary examples/repro-normals/noaa_normals_repro.ffl \
           --library facetwork/ffl/file.ffl \
           --workflow repro.normals.ReproduceManyStations

The recomputation is an FFL script block bound to fw.file.FileOps — no handler and no domain package. The ~18 MB daily file is read inside the script rather than passed through a step payload, which would exceed the 16 MB document limit. foreach … limit 4 bounds how many stations are in flight so the run does not pull twelve large files from NOAA at once.

6. Limitations, and a correction

This page previously reported "four stations do not reproduce at all". That was wrong — an artifact of comparing with a relative tolerance. Fahrenheit is an interval scale: its zero is arbitrary, so "within 2% of the value" has no physical meaning. Chicago's recomputed TAVG is off by a near-constant −0.9 °F all year, and 2% of the reading is 0.50 °F in January but 1.55 °F in July:

January  NOAA 25.2  2% = 0.50  → FAIL
July     NOAA 77.5  2% = 1.55  → pass

Same error, opposite verdict, purely seasonal. The comparison gained an abs_tolerance for interval scales and the study was re-run. The station-level bias findings are unchanged — they come from the sign test, which has no threshold — but the pass/fail split is gone, replaced by the error distribution above, which was always the more honest presentation.
  • Our method is deliberately naive. A plain monthly mean is not NOAA's documented procedure, which includes estimation and homogenisation. This measures the gap between the archive and the product; it does not claim the published normals are wrong. Where they differ, the published value is the more considered one.
  • Twelve stations, all US first-order airport/city sites. The pattern may not extend to cooperative-observer stations, which have different reporting practices and more missing data.
  • TAVG is computed as (TMAX+TMIN)/2, the conventional definition but not necessarily how the published TAVG normal is derived.
  • The sign test detects that residuals are one-sided. It does not attribute a cause; the station-specific explanations offered above are consistent with the data, not demonstrated by it.
