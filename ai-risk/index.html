How fast are the drivers of AI risk moving?

Nobody can measure the probability that AI destroys humanity. What can be measured is how quickly the things that would drive such a catastrophe are changing: how powerful AI systems are, how long they work without supervision, how good they are at hacking, how much paid work they can do, how much money is racing in, and how many harms are already being reported. This board tracks 8 of those indicators from open data and asks of each one what you would ask of a clock: is it moving faster or slower than it was?

Right now: 3 of 8 indicators are accelerating (Offensive cyber skill (Cybench), Private investment in AI, Cost of a frontier training run), 2 are slowing (Training compute of frontier models, Expert science knowledge (GPQA Diamond)), and the rest are steady or too new to call. AI researchers surveyed in 2023 put a median 5% chance on an outcome as bad as human extinction. Read the paper for what each indicator means and what it cannot tell you.

Outages and hacks blamed on AI

Curated from news reports, as of 2026-11-23. Each row keeps apart what happened, the cause the organisation states, and who links it to AI.

Between 12 and 17 November 2026, 5 large outages hit in a row: Microsoft, FAA, JPMorgan Chase, Apple, NSA. None has an official AI cause. Each organisation blames its own bug, update, maintenance or router, and officials say none was a cyberattack and that they are not connected. The AI explanations range from speculation, such as the Homeland Security Secretary passing on a theory that "it's the SI" (superintelligence), to an anonymously sourced claim that rushed patches for flaws found by AI overloaded systems. The Director of National Intelligence tied the NSA outage to patching for a PHP vulnerability. AI-assisted research had found PHP flaws that autumn.

The clearest AI harms of the year are elsewhere: in 2 cases an AI system acted on its own and its maker said so, and in 3 investigators, the FBI among them, say attackers used AI tools to do the work. These are events, not a rate, so they are not on the pace board. They are what the hacking benchmark measures in the lab, showing up in the world.

Did the AI blame hold up?

20 early public claims that AI caused or enabled an outage, breach or hack, and what the official account said later. Curated 2026-11-24; open claims are checked again each week.

Of the 6 resolved claims made by officials or commentators without evidence, none held up. Of the 8 resolved claims that came with direct evidence (logs, screenshots, code, the malware itself), 7 held up fully or partly. Claims resting on anonymous sources usually held up in part: AI was involved, but not the way the first report said.

This is a small, hand-built sample, and it is not random: it holds the cases that got press coverage and that a search could find. Read it as a first measurement, not a rate. The official account is usually the organisation's own, so a "did not hold up" often means "the organisation that would be blamed says no". Where both dates are known (14 claims), the claim and the official account were a median of 2 days apart. In 3 of the claims made without evidence, the organisation had already named an ordinary cause before the claim was made.

How to read this. Every number is recomputed from the sources on each refresh. A board full of red does not mean doom is near, and a green one does not mean safety: it says how fast the inputs to the risk are moving.
