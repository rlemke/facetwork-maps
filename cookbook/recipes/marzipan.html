Marzipan

A smooth almond paste scented with rose water and bitter almond, for shaping into little fruits or dipping in chocolate.

Dairy-free 1.1 g net carbs Serves 16 · 1 piece (about 20 g) prep 20 min · rest 1 h

Ingredients

  • 200 g almond flour (blanched, finely ground)
  • 100 g powdered erythritol (confectioners' style)
  • 1 large egg white, pasteurised
  • ½ tsp almond extract
  • 1 tsp rose water
  • 0.0625 tsp fine salt

Not low-carb is a rough estimate of a conventional version, not a tested recipe: powdered sugar for powdered erythritol (confectioners' style) (0.7 × by weight). Everything else is as written. Sweeteners are matched for sweetness, and ordinary recipes are often sweeter, so the real difference is usually larger.

Contains: eggs, tree nuts.

From typical product labels, which vary by brand: almond extract, powdered erythritol (confectioners' style), rose water.

Method

  1. Pulse the almond flour and powdered erythritol in a food processor until very fine, about 30 seconds; stop before it turns oily.
  2. Add the egg white, almond extract, rose water and salt and pulse until the mixture gathers into a ball.
  3. Turn out and knead briefly until smooth. If it is sticky, knead in a little more almond flour; if crumbly, a few drops of water.
  4. Wrap and chill 1 hour, then shape into small balls, fruits or logs. Store airtight in the fridge.

Dairy-free as written

Tips

  • Use a pasteurised egg white, since the marzipan is not cooked.
  • Commercial marzipan is half sugar, which holds its shape; this version is softer, so keep shapes small and chill them.

Where it comes from

Marzipan · Toledo · Southern Europe · medieval

Sweet pastes of almonds and sugar probably reached Europe from the Middle East in the Middle Ages. Toledo in Spain, whose convents are long famous for mazapán, and the German port of Lübeck, which became a great marzipan centre, both claim it as their own; neither claim can be settled, and the sweet was made widely around the Mediterranean early on.
