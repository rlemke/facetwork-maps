Eggnog

Thick, nutmeg-scented cooked egg custard drink, gently heated for safety and served cold.

Dairy-free option 5.6 g net carbs Serves 6 · ⅔ cup prep 10 min · cook 10 min · rest 3 h

Ingredients

  • 4 large egg yolks
  • ⅓ cup granulated erythritol (63 g)
  • 2 cups whole milk (488 g)
  • 1 cup heavy whipping cream (238 g)
  • ½ tsp ground nutmeg, freshly grated if possible, plus extra to serve
  • ⅛ tsp ground cinnamon
  • 0.0625 tsp fine salt
  • 1 tsp pure vanilla extract
  • 1 tsp rum extract, or 60–90 ml (¼–⅓ cup) dark rum or brandy

Method

  1. Whisk the egg yolks and erythritol in a bowl until pale and thick.
  2. Heat the milk, cream, nutmeg, cinnamon and salt in a pan until steaming.
  3. Pour the hot milk into the yolks in a thin stream, whisking all the time, then return everything to the pan.
  4. Cook over low heat, stirring constantly with a spatula, until the mixture thickens slightly and reaches 71 °C (160 °F) on a thermometer. Do not let it boil or it will curdle.
  5. Strain into a jug, stir in the vanilla and rum extract (or spirit), cool, then refrigerate at least 3 hours.
  6. Whisk before serving and grate a little nutmeg over each glass.

Dairy-free version

  • Use unsweetened almond milk instead of whole milk.
  • Use coconut cream (canned, unsweetened) instead of heavy whipping cream.

With almond milk and coconut cream the eggnog is a little thinner and tastes of coconut; nutmeg and rum carry it well.

Tips

  • Erythritol can crystallise in the cold drink; allulose or a powdered sweetener stays dissolved.

Where it comes from

Eggnog · Britain and colonial America · Northern Europe · 1700s

Eggnog descends from British posset, a medieval drink of hot milk curdled with wine or ale and sometimes thickened with eggs. In the American colonies, where milk, eggs and rum were plentiful, a cold egg-and-milk punch made with rum became a holiday favourite, and the name eggnog was in use by the eighteenth century. The origin of the word nog itself is uncertain.
