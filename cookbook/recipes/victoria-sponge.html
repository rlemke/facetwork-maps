Victoria sponge

Two buttery almond sponges sandwiched with a quick raspberry chia jam and softly whipped cream, dusted on top.

Dairy-free option 3.9 g net carbs Serves 10 · 1 slice prep 30 min · cook 25 min · rest 30 min

Ingredients

Sponges

  • ½ cup unsalted butter (114 g), softened
  • ½ cup granulated erythritol (96 g)
  • 4 large eggs, at room temperature
  • 1½ tsp pure vanilla extract
  • 2 cups almond flour (blanched, finely ground) (192 g)
  • 2 tbsp coconut flour (14 g)
  • 2 tsp baking powder
  • ¼ tsp fine salt
  • 3 tbsp unsweetened almond milk (45 g)

Raspberry jam

  • 150 g raspberries
  • 2 tbsp granulated erythritol (24 g)
  • 1 tbsp chia seeds (12 g)

Filling

  • ⅔ cup heavy whipping cream (159 g), cold
  • 1 tbsp powdered erythritol (confectioners' style)
  • 1 tbsp powdered erythritol (confectioners' style), for dusting

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 175 °C (350 °F). Grease two 20 cm (8 in) round pans and line the bases with parchment.
  2. Simmer the raspberries and erythritol for 5 minutes, mashing them, then stir in the chia seeds and leave to thicken while you make the cake.
  3. Beat the butter and erythritol for 3 minutes until light. Beat in the eggs one at a time, then the vanilla.
  4. Whisk the almond flour, coconut flour, baking powder and salt together and fold them in, then fold in the almond milk to a soft dropping consistency.
  5. Divide between the pans, level, and bake 20–25 minutes, until golden and springy. Cool 10 minutes, then turn out onto a rack and cool completely; almond sponges are fragile while warm.
  6. Whip the cream with 1 tbsp powdered erythritol to soft peaks.
  7. Spread the jam over one sponge, then the cream, and set the second sponge on top. Dust with the remaining powdered erythritol.

Dairy-free version

  • Use dairy-free butter (stick, plant-based) instead of unsalted butter.
  • Use coconut cream (canned, unsweetened) instead of heavy whipping cream — chilled, firm part only.

Dairy-free butter gives a slightly less rich crumb, and whipped coconut cream is softer, so fill the cake shortly before serving.

Tips

  • For the classic jam-only version, leave out the cream and use a little more jam.

Where it comes from

Sponge cake · England · Northern Europe · 1800s

The cake is named after Queen Victoria, who is said to have enjoyed sponge cake with her afternoon tea; the link to the queen herself is traditional rather than documented. Reliable baking powder from the mid-nineteenth century made a light, raised butter sponge practical, and a jam-filled sandwich of two such layers became a fixture of the English tea table.
