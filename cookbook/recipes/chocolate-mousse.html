Chocolate mousse

The bistro mousse made the old way: melted chocolate, egg yolks and beaten whites, nothing else to hide behind.

Dairy-free 3.3 g net carbs Serves 6 · 1 small glass (about 1/2 cup) prep 25 min · cook 5 min · rest 4 h

Ingredients

  • 115 g unsweetened baking chocolate (100% cacao), chopped
  • 2 tbsp coconut oil (27 g)
  • 3 tbsp strong brewed coffee or espresso (45 g), hot
  • ½ cup powdered erythritol (confectioners' style) (60 g)
  • 4 large egg yolks, at room temperature
  • 1 tsp pure vanilla extract
  • ⅛ tsp fine salt
  • 4 large egg whites, at room temperature
  • ¼ tsp cream of tartar
  • 10 drops liquid stevia, optional, to taste

Everything else is as written. Sweeteners are matched for sweetness, and ordinary recipes are often sweeter, so the real difference is usually larger.

From typical product labels, which vary by brand: liquid stevia, powdered erythritol (confectioners' style), unsweetened baking chocolate (100% cacao).

Method

  1. Melt the chocolate and coconut oil together in a heatproof bowl set over barely simmering water, stirring until smooth. Take the bowl off the heat.
  2. Whisk in the hot coffee and all but 2 tbsp of the powdered erythritol. The mixture will tighten, then loosen again as you keep whisking.
  3. Let it cool for 5 minutes, then whisk in the yolks one at a time, followed by the vanilla, salt and stevia. Taste: unsweetened chocolate needs the sweetener adjusted to your liking now, before the whites go in.
  4. In a clean bowl, whip the whites with the cream of tartar to soft peaks, then add the remaining 2 tbsp erythritol and whip to firm, glossy peaks.
  5. Stir a quarter of the whites into the chocolate to lighten it, then fold in the rest in two additions with a large spatula, stopping as soon as no white streaks remain.
  6. Spoon into six small glasses and refrigerate at least 4 hours, until set.

Dairy-free as written

Tips

  • The eggs are not cooked; use very fresh eggs, or pasteurised eggs if serving anyone vulnerable.
  • The chocolate must be warm but not hot when the yolks go in, or they will scramble; just warm to the lip is right.

Where it comes from

Chocolate mousse · Paris · Western Europe · 1700s

French cookery books of the 1700s already describe light, whipped chocolate preparations under the name mousse. The plain version of chocolate folded with beaten eggs became a fixture of French home and bistro cooking in the twentieth century, and from there spread worldwide.
