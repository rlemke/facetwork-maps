Sugar-free maple-style syrup

A pourable pancake syrup of lightly caramelised allulose and maple extract, with the colour and roasted sweetness of maple syrup but no maple sugar.

Dairy-free 0.0 g net carbs Serves 10 · 2 tbsp prep 2 min · cook 12 min · rest 15 min

Ingredients

  • 1 cup allulose (192 g), divided
  • ¾ cup water (178 g), divided
  • ⅛ tsp fine salt
  • ⅛ tsp xanthan gum
  • 1 tsp maple extract
  • ½ tsp pure vanilla extract
  • ⅛ tsp ground cinnamon, optional, a tiny pinch

Not low-carb is a rough estimate of a conventional version, not a tested recipe: maple syrup for allulose (1.4 × by weight); no water; no xanthan gum. Everything else is as written. Sweeteners are matched for sweetness, and ordinary recipes are often sweeter, so the real difference is usually larger.

Contains: none of the common allergen groups.

From typical product labels, which vary by brand: allulose, maple extract, water, xanthan gum.

Method

  1. Put a third of the allulose and 1 tbsp of the water in a heavy saucepan and cook over medium-low heat, swirling rather than stirring, until it turns a deep amber, 5–8 minutes. Allulose browns quickly, so watch it closely.
  2. Take the pan off the heat and very carefully pour in the rest of the water; it will spit and seize.
  3. Return to low heat, add the remaining allulose and the salt, and stir until the hardened caramel has dissolved and the syrup is clear, 3–4 minutes.
  4. Sprinkle the xanthan gum over the surface while whisking hard so it does not clump, then simmer 1 minute more.
  5. Off the heat, stir in the maple extract, vanilla and the cinnamon, if using. Cool 15 minutes; it thickens as it cools. Refrigerate up to 1 month and warm before serving.

Dairy-free as written

Tips

  • Maple extracts vary in strength; add half, taste, and add the rest if needed.
  • Too thick when cold? Stir in a spoonful of hot water.

Where it comes from

Maple syrup (imitation) · Quebec · North America · pre-colonial

This sugar-free syrup imitates maple syrup; its flavour comes from maple extract, not maple sugar. Indigenous peoples of northeastern North America were boiling maple sap into syrup and sugar before Europeans arrived, and European settlers learned the practice from them. Quebec is today by far the largest producer of maple syrup.
