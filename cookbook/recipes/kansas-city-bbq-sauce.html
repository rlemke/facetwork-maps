Kansas City barbecue sauce

A thick, glossy, sweet-and-tangy tomato barbecue sauce built on tomato paste and allulose, with a spoonful of molasses for the brown-sugar note.

Dairy-free 2.2 g net carbs Serves 20 · 2 tbsp prep 5 min · cook 25 min

Ingredients

  • 170 g tomato paste, one 170 g (6 oz) can
  • 1 cup water (237 g)
  • ⅓ cup apple cider vinegar (79 g)
  • ⅓ cup allulose (63 g)
  • 2 tbsp granulated erythritol (24 g)
  • 2 tsp blackstrap or dark molasses (13 g)
  • 1 tbsp Worcestershire sauce (17 g)
  • 1 tbsp prepared mustard (yellow or Dijon) (15 g)
  • 2 tsp sweet or smoked paprika, smoked
  • 1 tsp onion powder
  • 1 tsp garlic powder
  • 1 tsp chili powder
  • ½ tsp ground black pepper
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • ¼ tsp cayenne pepper or chili flakes
  • ½ tsp liquid smoke, optional

Contains: fish.

From typical product labels, which vary by brand: allulose, granulated erythritol, liquid smoke, water.

Method

  1. Whisk everything except the liquid smoke in a small saucepan until no streaks of tomato paste remain.
  2. Bring to a gentle simmer over medium-low heat and cook, uncovered, 20–25 minutes, stirring often and scraping the bottom: allulose scorches faster than sugar.
  3. When the sauce coats a spoon thickly, take it off the heat and stir in the liquid smoke, if using. Taste and adjust salt, vinegar and heat.
  4. Brush onto ribs, chicken or pork only in the last 10–15 minutes of cooking so it glazes without burning, and serve the rest on the side.
  5. Cool and refrigerate in a clean jar for up to 2 weeks.

Dairy-free as written

Tips

  • The 2 tsp of molasses is there only for flavour (it is sugar, about 10 g of carbs in the whole batch); leave it out and the sauce is about 0.5 g lower per serving.
  • Worcestershire contains anchovy (fish allergen); use 1 tbsp tamari instead if needed.
  • The sauce thickens further as it cools; loosen with a spoonful of water when reheating.

On the menu for: Outdoor barbecue.

Where it comes from

Barbecue sauce · Kansas City, Missouri · North America · 1900s

Kansas City's barbecue tradition is usually traced to Henry Perry, who sold slow-smoked meats in the city from the early 1900s. The city became known for meats served with a thick, sweet, tomato-and-molasses sauce, and bottled sauces in that style spread it across the United States in the later twentieth century, so that for many Americans it is simply what barbecue sauce means.
