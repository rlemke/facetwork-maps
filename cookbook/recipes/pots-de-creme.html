Chocolate pots de crème

Small, dense chocolate custards baked in a water bath; richer and smoother than a mousse.

Dairy-free option 4.1 g net carbs Serves 6 · 1 pot prep 20 min · cook 35 min · rest 4 h

Ingredients

  • 1½ cups heavy whipping cream (357 g)
  • ½ cup unsweetened almond milk (120 g)
  • 70 g unsweetened baking chocolate (100% cacao), finely chopped
  • 5 large egg yolks
  • ⅓ cup granulated erythritol (63 g)
  • 1 tsp pure vanilla extract
  • ⅛ tsp fine salt
  • ¼ tsp instant espresso powder

Not low-carb is a rough estimate of a conventional version, not a tested recipe: sugar for granulated erythritol (0.7 × by weight). Everything else is as written. Sweeteners are matched for sweetness, and ordinary recipes are often sweeter, so the real difference is usually larger.

Contains: dairy, eggs, tree nuts. Dairy-free version contains: coconut, eggs, tree nuts.

From typical product labels, which vary by brand: granulated erythritol, unsweetened baking chocolate (100% cacao).

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 150 °C (300 °F). Set six small ramekins or pots in a roasting tin.
  2. Heat the cream and almond milk until steaming, pour over the chocolate and leave 2 minutes, then stir until smooth.
  3. Whisk the yolks, erythritol, vanilla, salt and espresso powder. Slowly whisk in the chocolate cream and strain into a jug.
  4. Pour into the pots and add hot water to the tin to come halfway up. Cover the tin loosely with foil.
  5. Bake 30–35 minutes, until set at the edge with a slight wobble in the centre. Cool, then refrigerate at least 4 hours.

Dairy-free version

  • Use coconut cream (canned, unsweetened) instead of heavy whipping cream.

Coconut cream makes an equally dense custard; the chocolate covers most of the coconut flavour.

Tips

  • Unsweetened chocolate keeps the carbs low; taste the warm chocolate cream and add a few drops of stevia if you like it sweeter.

Where it comes from

Baked custard · France · Western Europe · 1700s

Pots de crème are named for the small lidded cups they were baked and served in. French porcelain makers were producing sets of these custard cups by the 1700s, a sign of how fashionable the little custards were at table. Vanilla, coffee and chocolate are the classic flavours.
