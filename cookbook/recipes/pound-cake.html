Pound cake

A plain, close-textured butter loaf built on the old equal-weights idea, with almond flour carrying the structure.

Dairy-free option 2.7 g net carbs Serves 12 · 1 slice prep 20 min · cook 1 h · rest 30 min

Ingredients

  • 225 g unsalted butter, softened
  • 200 g granulated erythritol
  • 4 large eggs, at room temperature
  • 2 tsp pure vanilla extract
  • 1 tsp lemon zest
  • 225 g almond flour (blanched, finely ground)
  • 20 g coconut flour
  • 1½ tsp baking powder
  • ½ tsp fine salt
  • ¼ cup sour cream (58 g)

Everything else is as written. Sweeteners are matched for sweetness, and ordinary recipes are often sweeter, so the real difference is usually larger.

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 165 °C (325 °F). Grease a 23 × 13 cm (9 × 5 in) loaf pan and line it with a parchment sling.
  2. Beat the butter and erythritol for 4–5 minutes, until very pale and fluffy; this creaming is what lifts the cake.
  3. Beat in the eggs one at a time, scraping the bowl after each, then the vanilla and lemon zest.
  4. Whisk the almond flour, coconut flour, baking powder and salt together. Fold half into the batter, then the sour cream, then the rest.
  5. Scrape into the pan, level, and bake 55–60 minutes, until golden and a skewer comes out clean. Cover with foil for the last 20 minutes if it browns quickly.
  6. Cool in the pan 20 minutes, then lift out and cool completely before slicing.

Dairy-free version

  • Use dairy-free butter (stick, plant-based) instead of unsalted butter.
  • Use unsweetened coconut yogurt instead of sour cream.

Use a firm stick-style dairy-free butter; a soft tub spread will not cream and the loaf turns dense and greasy.

Tips

  • Erythritol can recrystallise on standing; a powdered sweetener in the batter gives a smoother crumb the next day.

Where it comes from

Butter cake · England · Northern Europe · 1700s

Pound cake is named for its original proportions: a pound each of flour, butter, sugar and eggs. Recipes appear in English cookbooks by the eighteenth century, and the cake travelled to the American colonies, where it became a Southern classic. Later versions lightened it with chemical leaveners and changed the proportions, but the name stuck.
