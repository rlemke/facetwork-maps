Strawberries Romanoff

Strawberries steeped in orange juice and zest, folded through a tangy sweetened cream.

Dairy-free option 5.9 g net carbs Serves 6 · 1 glass prep 15 min · rest 1 h

Ingredients

Strawberries

  • 450 g strawberries, hulled and halved or quartered
  • 2 tbsp fresh orange juice (30 g)
  • 1 tsp orange zest
  • 2 tbsp granulated erythritol (24 g)

Cream

  • ½ cup heavy whipping cream (119 g), cold
  • ¼ cup sour cream (58 g)
  • 2 tbsp powdered erythritol (confectioners' style) (16 g)
  • 1 tsp pure vanilla extract

Everything else is as written. Sweeteners are matched for sweetness, and ordinary recipes are often sweeter, so the real difference is usually larger.

Contains: dairy. Dairy-free version contains: coconut.

From typical product labels, which vary by brand: granulated erythritol, powdered erythritol (confectioners' style).

Method

  1. Toss the strawberries with the orange juice, zest and erythritol. Cover and refrigerate for 1 hour, stirring once, so they release their juice.
  2. Whip the cream with the powdered erythritol and vanilla to soft peaks, then fold in the sour cream.
  3. Set aside a few berries for the top. Fold the rest, with a spoonful of their juice, gently through the cream so it streaks pink.
  4. Spoon into 6 glasses, top with the reserved berries and serve within an hour.

Dairy-free version

  • Use coconut cream (canned, unsweetened) instead of heavy whipping cream — the solid part of a well-chilled can.
  • Use unsweetened coconut yogurt instead of sour cream.

Coconut cream and coconut yogurt make a looser, softer cream with a clear coconut flavour; fold it together just before serving.

Tips

  • The classic recipe adds an orange liqueur to the berries; a teaspoon per serving is traditional but adds sugar.

Where it comes from

Strawberries Romanoff · Paris, France · Western Europe · 1800s

The dish is named for the Romanov dynasty of Russia and is often credited to French chefs of the nineteenth century who cooked for Russian aristocrats, though no single inventor is firmly documented. It became widely known in the United States in the mid-twentieth century, when it was associated with fashionable restaurants. The map marker reflects its usual attribution to French cookery.
